The book is a compilation of tactics from the Chinese general Sun Tzu.
# Vocabulary
* ==**Protracted**==Lasting a long time (or longer than expected and or desired).
* ==**War material**==: "includes all the impedimenta of an army, apart from provisions"
* ==**Exchequer**==: a royal or national treasury.
* ==**Exactions**==: the action of demanding and obtaining something from someone, especially a payment or service.
* ==**Arbiter**==: a person who settles a dispute or has ultimate authority in a matter.
* ==**Ostensibly**==: Seemingly but perhaps not actually.
* ==**Li**==: 500 meters.
# Laying Plans
### The Five Constant Factors on the Field
* ==**The Moral Law**==: A principle of harmony, similar to the [[Taoism|Tao]]. This causes the people to be in complete accord with their ruler, so that they will follow them regardless of their lives, undismayed by any danger.
* ==**Heaven**==: "Signifies night and day, cold and heat, times and seasons." One could say that these are the [[Critique of Pure Reason#Introductory Vocabulary|phenomena]] corresponding to ==**weather conditions**== one could find in the battlefield.
* ==**Earth**==: "comprises distances, great and small; danger and security; open ground and narrow passes; the chances of life and death.", This is the use of the form of terrain.
* ==**The Commander**==: "stands for the virtues of wisdom, sincerely, benevolence, courage and strictness.", this can be responding to the five cardinal virtues of the Chinese
	1. <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">Humanity or Benevolance</mark> : 
	2. <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">Uprightness of Mind</mark>: This replaces Courage and strictness
	3. <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">Self-Respect, Self-Control</mark>: "proper feeling", This replaces Courage and strictness
	4. <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">Wisdom</mark>:
	5. <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">Sincerity or Good Faith</mark>:
* ==**Method and Discipline**==: "are to be understood the marshaling of the army in its proper subdivisions, the graduations of rank among the officers, the maintenance of roads by which supplies may reach the army, and the control of military expenditure."
When determining military conditions one should ask themselves:
1. Which of the two sovereigns is imbued with the **Moral Law**?
2. Which of the two generals has most ability?
3. With whom lie the advantages derived from Heaven and Earth?
4. On which side is discipline most rigorously enforced?
5. Which army is stronger?
	* Morally
	* Physically
6. On which side are officers more highly trained?
7. In which army is there the greater constancy both in reward and punishment?
Another idea they put forth is "While heading the profit of my counsel, avail yourself also of any helpful circumstances over and beyond the ordinary rules. ==**According as circumstances are favorable, one should modify one’s plans**==.", That is to say you should not pin your faith on abstract principles, since even though the laws of strategy can be clearly stated to benefit everyone, ==**you should instead be guided by the actions of the enemy**== in actual warfare.

> *All warfare is based on deception - Sun Tzu*

* ==**Ways to deceive the enemy**==:
	* When able to attack $\Rightarrow$ Seem unable to attack
	* When using forces $\Rightarrow$  appear inactive
	* When being near $\Rightarrow$ Appear as though we are far away.
	* When we are far $\Rightarrow$ Make it appear as if we are near.
	* Hold out baits to entice the enemy, feign disorder to crush them.
* ==**If they are secure at all points**==: Prepare and evade him.
* ==**If your opponent is of choleric (irritable) temper**==: seek to irritate him. Pretend to be weak, that he may grow arrogant.
* ==**If their forces are united**==: separate them
* "Now the general who wins a battle makes many calculations in his temple where the battle is fought."

# Waging War

> *He who wishes to fight must first count the cost - Ts‘ao Kung*

"In the operations of war, where there are in the field a thousand swift chariots, as many heavy chariots, and a hundred thousand ==**mailclad (chainmail)**== soldiers," $$\Rightarrow$$ "When you engage in actual fighting, if victory is long in coming, then men’s weapons will grow dull and their ardor will be damped. If you lay siege to a town, ==**you will exhaust your strength**==."
In simple terms: If the campaign is ==**protracted**== then the resources of the state will not equal the strain. Once your resources exhausted, other chieftains will take advantage of  the opportunity and you will not be able to avert the consequences that must ensue.
> *There is no instance of a country having benefited from prolonged warfare. - Sun Tzu*

> It is only one who is thoroughly acquainted with the evils of war that can thoroughly understand the profitable way of carrying it on. - Sun Tzu
* "==**That is, with rapidity**==. Only one who knows the disastrous effects of a long war can realize the supreme importance of rapidity in bringing it to a close"
> *The skillful soldier does not raise a second levy, neither are his supplywagons loaded more than twice. - Sun Tzu*
* Although a radical idea to recommend a soldier not come back for supplies, it is meant to highlight the importance of ==**speed in operation**== and ==**using as little of your home turfs supplies**==. This is made clearer in his next passage:
> *Bring war material with you from home, but forage on the enemy. Thus the army will have food enough for its needs. - Sun Tzu*
* Basically enforcing the fact that it is better to use the spoils of war when on the field.
> Poverty of the State exchequer causes an army to be maintained by contributions from a distance. Contributing to maintain an army at a distance causes the people to be impoverished.
* When the states funds have depleted, the army will have to start gaining these contributions from other sources, such as the people of the state. This goes back to the statement of prolonged warfare, where such an event leads to the impoverishment of the countries people as stated by the following passage.
> When their substance is drained away, the peasantry will be afflicted by heavy exactions.
* This quote again leads back to making the point of foraging on foreign land.
> Now in order to kill the enemy, our men must be roused to anger; that there may be advantage from defeating the enemy, they must have their rewards.
* This quote apart from making the point of foraging on foreign land, it also makes the point of boosting morale through rewards from the spoils of war.
> *Therefore in chariot fighting, when ten or more chariots have been taken, those should be rewarded who took the first. Our own flags should be substituted for those of the enemy, and the chariots mingled and used in conjunction with ours. The captured soldiers should be kindly treated and kept. - Sun Tzu*
* Rewarding soldiers for their conquest is a double edged sword, it could lead to them losing sight of what the purpose of the war is. ==**You must keep your humanity when in war by treating prisoners humanely**==. This is in hopes that maybe they will also do the same as ==**the leader of armies is the arbiter of the peoples fate**==. This is also following the Confucian Principle of, "treating others as one would want to be treated by them".
# Attack by Stratagem
> *In the practical art of war, the best thing of all is to take the enemy’s country whole and intact; to shatter and destroy it is not so good. So, too, it is better to recapture an army entire than to destroy it, to capture a regiment, a detachment or a company entire than to destroy them. - Sun Tzu*

> *Hence to fight and conquer in all your battles is not supreme excellence; supreme excellence consists in breaking the enemy’s resistance without fighting. - Sun Tzu*

In these two quotes we can see how Sun Tzu recommends above all to avoid conflict.
> Thus the highest form of generalship is to balk the enemy’s plans
* Here they speak of counter-attacking. When an enemy already has a plan of attack laid out, attack before them as so that attack strategy cannot be held.
* Isolate the enemy from their allies
> *The rule is, not to besiege walled cities if it can possibly be avoided. - Sun Tzu*
* This rule is somewhat comedic, as it implores generals to stop attacking well fortified, walled cities. They mention in their arguments that the preparation of ==**mantlets**== (large shields), moveable shelters, and various implements will take up 3 months. and the piling of mounds another 3 months. It is obvious this goes against Sun Tzu's ruling of prolonged warfare. Here is also mentioned that, "The general, unable to control his irritation, will launch his men to the assault like swarming ants", **Going back to his statement that if your opponent is ill tempered, irritate them**. 
> *Therefore the skillful leader subdues the enemy’s troops without any fighting; he captures their cities without laying siege to them; he overthrows their kingdom without lengthy operations in the field. - Sun Tzu*

> *With his forces intact he will dispute the mastery of the Empire, and thus, without losing a man, his triumph will be complete. - Sun Tzu*
* Here scheming and wit seems like a better alternative to sieging a city. This would be attacking by stratagem.
### Fight or Flight?
* If your forces are ten to your enemies one, then surround them
* If your forces are five to your enemies one, then attack them
* If twice as numerous as your enemy, then divide your army into two
* If equally matched, then offer a battle
* If slightly inferior, then avoid the enemy
* If unequal in every way, then flee from them
### Methods by which a ruler brings misfortune to their army
1. "By commanding the army to advance or to retreat, being ignorant of the fact that it cannot obey. This is called hobbling the army."
2. "By attempting to govern an army in the same way as he administers a kingdom, being ignorant of the conditions which obtain in an army. This causes restlessness in the soldier’s minds."
3. "By employing the officers of his army without discrimination." (right person for the job)
### Five essentials for Victory
Who will win is:
1. Who knows when to fight and when not to fight.
2. Who knows how to handle both superior and inferior forces
3. Whose army is animated by the same spirit throughout all its ranks
4. who prepared themselves, and waits to take the enemy unprepared.
5. who has military capacity and is not interfered with by the sovereign.
	* This final point is stating that the sovereign (ruler) should give broad instructions, but the person who decides on the battle is the general.
> *If you know the enemy and know yourself, you need not fear the result of a hundred battles. If you know yourself but not the enemy, for every victory gained you will also suffer a defeat. If you know neither the enemy nor yourself, you will succumb in every battle. - Sun Tzu*

> *Knowing the enemy enables you to take the offensive, knowing yourself enables you to stand on the defensive. Attack is the secret of defense; defense is the planning of an attack. - Chang Yu*
# Tactical Dispositions
> *The good fighters of old first put themselves beyond the possibility of defeat, and then waited for an opportunity of defeating the enemy. - Sun Tzu*
* We have the ability to secure ourselves from defeat, and the ability to defeat the enemy is provided by the enemy (a blunder).
> *Thus the good fighter is able to secure himself against defeat, but cannot make certain of defeating the enemy. - Sun Tzu*

> *One may **know** how to conquer without being able to **do** it.*

> The general who is skilled in defense hides in the most secret recesses of the earth;
* Hides in such a way the enemy has no clue of there whereabouts
> who is skilled in attack flashes forth from the topmost heights of heaven.
* Attacks in such a way the enemy has no time to prepare.
> *What the ancients called a clever fighter is one who not only wins, but excels in winning with ease. ... Hence the skillful fighter puts himself into a position which makes defeat impossible, and does not miss the moment for defeating the enemy.*

## Cheng and Ch'i
* <mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">Cheng</mark>: Is facing the enemy.
	* Enforces passivity, or waiting for the right opportunity
* <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">Ch'i</mark>: Is making a lateral diversion.
	* Is to be active
> *We must cause the enemy to regard our straightforward attack as one that is secretly designed, and vice versa; thus CHENG may also be CH‘I, and CH‘I may also be CHENG. - Ho Shih*
### Example
Han Hsin marched ==**ostensibly**== against Lin-chin, when they suddenly threw a large force across the Yellow River in wooden tubs, utterly disconcerting his opponent.
* <mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">The march on Lin-chin was CHENG</mark>
* <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">The surprise maneuver was Ch'I</mark>
==**That said military writers do not agree on the meaning of Cheng and Ch'i**==. Although an excerpt says:
> *A CH‘I maneuver may be CHENG, if we make the enemy look upon it as CHENG; then our real attack will be CH‘I, and vice versa. The whole secret lies in confusing the enemy, so that he cannot fathom our real intent. - T'ang Emperor T'ai Tsung*

> *In battle, there are not more than two methods of attack - the direct and the indirect; yet these two in combination give rise to an endless series of maneuvers. - Sun Tzu*
# Weak Points and Strong
> *Whoever is first in the field and awaits the coming of the enemy, will be fresh for the fight; whoever is second in the field and has to hasten to battle will arrive exhausted. - Sun Tzu*
* Here it's saying that a great soldier fights on their own terms or not at all. Instead of being at the will of the enemy, you must impose your will on the enemy. By holding out advantages to their enemy, you can make them approach of their own accord, and by inflicting damage, they can make it impossible for the enemy to draw near. Again there is a talk of counter-acting and deceiving the enemy.
> *If the enemy is taking his ease, he can harass him; if well supplied with food, he can starve him out; if quietly encamped, he can force him to move. - Sun Tzu*
* You can ensure the victory by attacking points that are undefended, or by simply waiting for the enemies resources to dwindle as they wait for you.
> *If we wish to fight, the enemy can be forced to an engagement even though he be sheltered behind a high rampart and a deep ditch. All we need do is attack some other place that he will be obliged to relieve. If we do not wish to fight, we can prevent the enemy from engaging us even though the lines of our encampment be merely traced out on the ground. All we need do is to throw something odd and unaccountable in his way. - Sun Tzu*
* Here he speaks of controlling the engagement of battle, and preventing the enemy from doing something they want to do.
> *For should the enemy strengthen his van, he will weaken his rear; should he strengthen his rear, he will weaken his van; should he strengthen his left, he will weaken his right; should he strengthen his right, he will weaken his left. If he sends reinforcements everywhere, he will everywhere be weak. - Sun Tzu*
* There is no point where you will be completely safe, it is recommended instead to defend against any point that might cause a decisive blow, and let small misfortunes fall on other places, to avoid greater ones.
> *Do not repeat the tactics which have gained you one victory, but let your methods be regulated by the infinite variety of circumstances. Military tactics are like unto water; for water in its natural course runs away from high places and hastens downwards. So in war, the way is to avoid what is strong and to strike at what is weak - Sun Tzu*
# Maneuvering
> *Maneuvering with an army is advantageous; with an undisciplined multitude, most dangerous. - Sun Tzu*
* This goes back to the first chapter, "Laying Plans", where it is important to have a disciplined and eager army.
> *If you set a fully equipped army in march in order to snatch an advantage, the chances are that you will be too late. On the other hand, to detach a flying column for the purpose involves the sacrifice of its baggage and stores. - Sun Tzu*
* Arriving early is important to secure an advantage, but supplies for the fight are also important. A recommendation they give instead is to reduce halts throughout the day and night, to try and cover double the distance, but this comes at the disadvantage of some soldiers possibly being left behind.
> *doing a ==**hundred Li**== in order to wrest an advantage, the leaders of all your ==**three divisions will fall into the hands of the enemy**==. The stronger men will be in front, the jaded ones will fall behind, and on this plan ==**only one-tenth of your army will reach its destination**==.*

> *If you march ==**fifty Li**== in order to outmaneuver the enemy, ==**you will lose the leader of your first division**==, and ==**only half your force will reach the goal**==.*

> *If you march ==**thirty Li**== with the same object, ==**two-thirds of your army will arrive**==.*

Its important to note the surroundings, the ==**Earth**==.
