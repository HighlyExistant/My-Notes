Given a point $p$ and a linear equation denoted by $y=mx+b$, derive the distance between the two. Of course the shortest distance between the two points would be along a perpendicular line to $mx+b$, therefore our point $p$ must lie on the line $-\frac{1}{m}x+b$. We can find this linear equation by doing: $$-\frac{1}{m}(x-p_x)+p_y$$which now gives us a linear equation to equal with the first. By doing this we can get the intersection: $$-\frac{1}{m}(x-p_x)+p_y=mx+b$$
This linear equation simplifies into giving us $$x=\frac{-m\left(b-p_y\right)+p_x}{m^{2}+1}$$
We will denote the $x$ on the previous equation as $I_x$, to make it clear that this denotes an intersection. This gives us that the intersection would be on point $$(I_x, mI_x+b)$$
And now we can obtain the Euclidean distance between these two points. $$\sqrt{(\frac{-m\left(b-p_y\right)+p_x}{m^{2}+1}-p.x)^2+m(\frac{-m\left(b-p_y\right)+p_x}{m^{2}+1})-p.y}$$
When simplified, it gives us this beautiful equation, such that $\alpha=b+mp.x-p.y$ and $\beta = (m^2+1)^{-1}$, we get an interesting coordinate $$(-m\alpha\beta,\alpha\beta)$$which is just the point of intersection displaced by the point you're trying to grab the distance from, and the distance being $$\sqrt{(m\alpha\beta)^2+(\alpha\beta)^2}$$Something to notice is that $\alpha$ is negative when on the left side of the line and positive on the right side. Its direction faces the same direction of the slope of the perpendicular line, or as the normal of the line with respect to where the point is (left or right of the line).
