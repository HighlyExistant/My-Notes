## <mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">1. Principle of Impartiality</mark>
> *For every rule proposed towards others, one is not exempt from that rule*

For any rule $r_P\in R_i$ (be it implicit or explicit), such that $R_i$ is the set of all rules set by an individual $i\in P$ placed upon any person $p\in P$ such that $P$ is the set of all persons, all persons $p\in P$ (including yourself) are not exempt from that rule.

In simpler terms: Any rules proposed by $i \in P$ must apply $\forall p\in P$.

This is very similar to Kant's 1st formulation of the categorical imperative, although I would consider it to be a weaker version of the categorical imperative, as the rules here $r$ are subjective to the person who is making those rules. The reason why I go for a weaker version of Kant's 1st formulation, is because this list of morality is supposed to be pragmatic (doable) as to how I view someone can live their lives. The principle of impartiality also has a few implications alongside it.
1. ==**It gets rid of hypocrisy**==. An individual $i\in P$ cannot make a rule $r\in R_i$ such that it only applies to $P-\{i\}$.
	1. This also implies that ==**Any interlocutor can use the logic of their opponents against them**==.
2. ==**It implies Hitchens Razor**==. If an individual $i$ makes up a fact, that implies that any individual $p\in P$ following the rules $R_i$ can make up a fact. In this case the fact they can make up is the inverse of the fact stated, which is equivalent to the dismissal of evidence.
>    *That which can be asserted without evidence can be dismissed without evidence*

# <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">3. Principle of Equivalent Context</mark>
## Language Formulation
> *For any two words that can be replaced within the same context, and still retain the same context those words should be treated as if they are equivalent.*

Given two groups of words $w_1$ and $w_2$ in sentence $S$, if $w_1$ can replace the word $w_2$ and still retain the same context $\mathcal{C}$ then $w_1\equiv w_2$. Example:
1. <mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">I love</mark> <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">you</mark> $\equiv$ <mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">I am in love with</mark> <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">you</mark>.
2. <mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">I hate</mark> <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">you</mark> $\equiv$ <mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">I feel an intense dislike for</mark> <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">you</mark> if the definition of $\text{hate}\in \mathcal{C}$ is $\text{hate}\to\text{intense dislike}$.
This applies to words that are fuzzily similar, such as synonyms, although they would not be exactly be equivalent, just somewhat equivalent.

Similarly this applies to made up words, as long as the context makes it so that they have the same meaning, such as:
1. Lowkenuinely $\equiv$ Lowkey, genuinely.
Its important to note that all words are made-up by us, it's the context which gives it meaning.

This principle does not only apply to words, but any means, such as for example, institutions, and money.
1. Fact 1: The minimum wage (currently $\$10.50$ per hour) is the minimum amount of money for someone to survive.
2. Fact 2: People in prison work in the prison to support their families outside of prison
3. Fact 3: The prison industrial complex pays workers $12$ cents per hour, or $96$ cents per 8 hours, meaning they are paid around 87.5 times less than minimum wage workers.
4. Theorem 1: The money paid in prisons is closer to $0$ cents than $\$10.50$.
5. Theorem 2: If $\$10.50$ is the amount of money needed to survive, and prisons pay workers around 87.5 times less than that, then the families outside will need other forms of support, rendering any prison labor as completely useless for the families outside.

7. Conclusion: The prison industrial complex is equivalent to a lighter version of slavery.
## Knowledge Formulation
> *For any action taken under an assumption, faulty or not, it means that you would have gone through with that action even under said assumption.*

Given two pieces of knowledge $K_i\in \mathcal{C}$, where $K_i$ is the knowledge taken under the action while $K_j\in \mathcal{C}_U$, is the actual knowledge present in the situation, then the action would still have taken place if $K_i=K_j$.

Let's say you had a persons $A$ and $B$ who are in a relationship. If person $A$ (who is 20) thinks that person $B$ is 17, when in reality they are also 20, then that means they would have gotten into the relationship even if $B$ was 17.
# <mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">4. Kant's Second Formulation of the Categorical Imperative</mark>
> *Act in such a way as to treat humanity, whether in your own person or in that of anyone else, always as an end and never merely as a means. - Immanuel Kant*

> *Treat human beings as ends to themselves - Immanuel Kant*

This second formulation also implies the following quote:
> *The liberty of one citizen ends where the liberty of another citizen begins" - Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes*
## Implications
* To view a human always as an end and never merely as a means, signifies that you view humans as autonomous beings in control of their own destiny.
	* Completely disavows any action which mistreats a human being (==**violates the liberty of another**==), such as murder, rape, sexual assault, abduction, torture, etc...
	* Doesn't allow bigotry to prevail, such as racism, sexism, homophobia, transphobia, xenophobia, etc...
	* Gives people the freedom of choice for ones self but not for others.
* By the mere fact that we exist, we have value in ourselves.
* Forms the golden rule: ==**you will treat others how you want to be treated**==.
## Will Imposition
* ==**Imposing Will**==: Given persons $A$ and $B$, if $A$ does an action unto $B$ without consent, then it is considered imposing ones will. To impose ones will, means to violate the liberties of another.
## Justification for Self-Defense
One would think that this principle would disavow any and every form of self defense, however it is justifiable to preserve your life over your victimizer, as long as you do only what you deemed to be necessary to preserve your own life. It is important to note that this principle not only avows the autonomy of other beings, but also oneself.
