Is the force acting on an object as it is touching the surface of another object. 
* ==**Static friction**==: is denoted as $f_s\leq\mu_s N$ where $\mu_s$ is the coefficient of static friction, and $N$ is the normal force of the surface acting on the mass $mg$, which can also be denoted as $F_N$. ==This type of friction is a scalar value, and holds no direction, as it simply opposes any force trying to move it==. **The normal force points perpendicular to the surface of contact**, that is to say that if an object is on a flat surface, the normal force will be pointing up proportional to the forces dragging the object in the opposite direction. It's **the force that prevents an object from sliding off a surface while its at rest (not moving)**. Something interesting about $\mu_s$ is that some of these common coefficients of friction [have been tabulated for ease of use](https://www.engineeringtoolbox.com/friction-coefficients-d_778.html).
* ==**Kinematic friction**==: is denoted as $f_k=\mu_{k}N$ where $N$ denotes the normal force $mg$ which denotes the [[Concepts#Weight|weight]] of the object. Kinematic friction starts being used when an object finally starts sliding off a surface, that is to say that the force $F$ that was sliding the object is now larger than $f_s$ and will then be using $f_k$ to see how much the object is pulled the other direction. It's important to note then that $f_k < f_s$ because the whole point of distinguishing between static and kinematic friction is that ==it takes more force to set an object in motion, than to keep it in motion==.
### Example
![[static_friction_example.svg | center]]
In the following image a person is trying to move a box with $F$ amount of force. Let's say that $f_s\leq5\text{N}$ and $F=3\text{N}$. The friction of the box with the ground will exert an equal and opposite force to $F$ which equals $-3\text{N}$, which will not allow it to move, because $F_{net}=0$. **It's not until $F>f_s$ when static friction is no longer in play and we enter to kinematic friction. This is why we represent static friction as an inequality**.
![[kinematic_friction_example.svg | center]]
Let's now say $F=6\text{N}$ and $f_k=3\text{N}$ and $f_s=5\text{N}$. We see that $F>f_s$ therefore we will now be dealing with $f_k$. due to $f_k$ going the opposite direction of $F$ we can subtract both of them giving us the final answer $F_{net}=3\text{N}$.
## Further Generalization
We notice that $F_N$ is pointing at $90^{\circ}$ because it is perpendicular to the inclination of $0^{\circ}$. Due to this fact it simplifies a lot of things making friction just be equal to $f=\mu N$. ==If we want to generalize to any type of inclination however, we need to adjust the force based on the inclination of the surface, therefore we add another value to the term== $cos(\theta)$ where $\theta$ is the angle of inclination of the surface. In the end this equals:
$$
	F=\mu Ncos(\theta)
$$

## Work done by Friction
Friction is constant so the work done by friction is just $W=-fd$ where $f$ is the frictional force. The reason why the work is negative is because friction resists motion.
